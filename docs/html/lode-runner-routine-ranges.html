Lode Runner Routine Ranges

Three places from the attract play, drawn the way edit() draws a routine: one row per basic block, the jumps as arrows in the gutter on the left. Each row shows only the instruction that ends its block, the leap the split reports record. The rest of each block is in edit().

entry
The address where a routine starts. Today there are 76.
range
From a routine's entry up to the next entry, by address. Only the entries decide it, nothing the run did.
span
For comparison: from a routine's lowest block to its highest. The blocks decide it.
band
The colored strip at the left edge of a row: the range the row lies in.
today in
The chip on the right: the routine that holds the block today. A chip outlined in red has another color than its band: address order and today's routines disagree there.

Group A: the range shows it

The jump goes backwards, below the jumper's own entry, into another routine's range.

Group B: the range can't see it

The jump stays inside the jumper's range. The RTS right in front of the target shows it.

Group A, case 1

TRY_GUARD_MOVE_DOWN jumps up into TRY_GUARD_MOVE_UP

TRY_GUARD_MOVE_DOWN ends with two JMPs up into the tail of TRY_GUARD_MOVE_UP: to 6f26 after a move within the row, to 6f29 after a move into the next row. TRY_GUARD_MOVE_UP's own entry, 6eac, never ran in the attract play.

Both targets lie below 6f39, so they are outside TRY_GUARD_MOVE_DOWN's range. By address they lie in MOVE_GUARD's range, but MOVE_GUARD never goes there.

Today: find_routines() follows the two jumps, because only one routine reaches the tail. So the five tail blocks belong to routine 6f39; the grade said 5 of its 19.

Rule on paper: a jump out of the range is not followed (part 5). No routine takes 6f26 in, so it starts a routine of its own (part 6). 6f29 joins it, because the return from the JSR at 6f26 lands there.

Group A, case 2

CHECK_FOR_INPUT branches down into CHECK_FOR_MODE_1_INPUT

CHECK_FOR_INPUT is one block. MOVE_PLAYER calls it with the JSR at 6595. Its BEQ jumped all 79 times, down to CHECK_FOR_MODE_1_INPUT, whose RTS returns to 6598, behind that JSR. A tail call made with a branch.

The target 69b8 lies below 6a12, outside CHECK_FOR_INPUT's range, in the range of TRY_DIGGING_LEFT_check_can_dig_left, which never goes there.

Today: the seven blocks of CHECK_FOR_MODE_1_INPUT belong to routine 6a12; the grade said 7 of its 8.

Rule on paper: the BEQ is not followed. No routine takes 69b8 in, so it starts a routine of its own, and the BEQ becomes an exit into it.

Group B, for contrast

MOVE_PLAYER jumps forward into TRY_MOVING_LEFT

The JMP at 65c8 goes forward, to 65d3. No routine starts between MOVE_PLAYER's entry 64bd and 65d3: the next entry is 67db. So the target lies inside MOVE_PLAYER's own range, and by address this looks like an ordinary jump inside the routine.

What shows it is the row right in front of the target. The block 65d2-65d3 is MOVE_PLAYER's RTS, and it ends exactly at 65d3. Nothing falls into 65d3: the only way in is the JMP.

Today: TRY_MOVING_LEFT's 16 blocks belong to routine 64bd, together with MOVE_PLAYER's 24. Band and chip agree everywhere, which is the point: the range gives no signal here.

Rule on paper, part 7: the target of a JMP that lies right behind a block ending in RTS, and is never fallen into, starts a routine. Backed by this one case. The risk: a loop head right behind one of the routine's own RTS blocks, reached only by JMP, would be cut off wrongly.
